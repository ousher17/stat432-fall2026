The value ||XTr||∞ should be very close to zero when the least-squares solution is computed correctly. This is because the residual vector is orthogonal to the columns of the design matrix at the least-squares optimum, so XTr = 0 in exact arithmetic. In practice it is tiny but not exactly zero because of numerical precision. This tells us that the residuals have no linear component in the column space of X, which is a defining property of least squares.

Question 2 (Gaussian likelihood for linear regression)

Continue with the data from Question 1. Suppose

Y | X ~ Nn(Xβ, σ2In).

The log-likelihood, including the constant term, is

ℓ(β, σ2) = -(n/2) log(2πσ2) - (1/(2σ2))(y − Xβ)T(y − Xβ).

a. For fixed σ2, explain why maximizing ℓ(β, σ2) over β is equivalent to minimizing the residual sum of squares. What does this imply about the MLE of β?

For fixed σ2, the only part of the Gaussian log-likelihood that depends on β is the quadratic term -(1 / (2σ2))(y − Xβ)T(y − Xβ). This is negative one-half times the residual sum of squares divided by σ2. Therefore, maximizing the likelihood is equivalent to minimizing the residual sum of squares. The maximum-likelihood estimate of β is the least-squares estimate, so the MLE matches the OLS solution when the Gaussian model is assumed.

b. For fixed β, differentiate the log-likelihood with respect to σ2 and derive the MLE. Calculate this estimate at β̂ and compare it with the unbiased estimate of σ2. Explain why the two denominators differ.

rss <- sum(r^2)
sigma2_MLE <- rss / n
sigma2_MLE
sigma2_unbiased <- rss / (n - ncol(X))
sigma2_unbiased

compare_variance <- data.frame(
  Estimate = c("MLE", "Unbiased"),
  Sigma2 = c(sigma2_MLE, sigma2_unbiased)
)
compare_variance

The MLE for σ2 is

σ̂MLE2 = RSS / n,

where RSS = (y − Xβ̂)T(y − Xβ̂). The unbiased estimate is

σ̂unbiased2 = RSS / (n − p)

with p = 6 regression coefficients including the intercept, so the denominator is n − 6. The MLE uses denominator n and is biased downward because it ignores the fact that the fitted coefficients consume degrees of freedom. The unbiased estimator corrects for this by accounting for the number of estimated parameters.

c. Let ex2 = (0,0,1,0,0,0)T. Using the variance estimate from part b, plot ℓ(β̂ + t ex2, σ̂MLE2) over a grid of t values from -1.25 to 1.25. State where the maximum occurs and explain why this agrees with the least-squares result.

The likelihood is maximized at t = 0, which corresponds to β̂. This is exactly the least-squares estimate, because for fixed σ2 the Gaussian log-likelihood is maximized when the residual sum of squares is minimized. Moving the coefficient away from its least-squares value increases the residual sum of squares and therefore lowers the likelihood.

Question 3 (Starting values in nonconvex optimization)

Consider the function

f(x) = exp(1.5x) - 3(x + 6)2 - 0.05x3,

with derivative

f'(x) = 1.5 exp(1.5x) - 6(x + 6) - 0.15 x2.

a. Plot f(x) over the interval [-40, 7]. Based on the plot, describe the important features of the objective function that may affect numerical optimization.

f <- function(x) {
  exp(1.5 * x) - 3 * (x + 6)^2 - 0.05 * x^3
}
f_prime <- function(x) {
  1.5 * exp(1.5 * x) - 6 * (x + 6) - 0.15 * x^2
}

curve(
  f,
  from = -40,
  to = 7,
  n = 1000,
  xlab = "x",
  ylab = "f(x)",
  main = "Objective Function"
)

The important feature is that the function is nonconvex and has multiple stationary points. In particular, it has multiple local minima separated by a local maximum. This can affect optimization because starting values may lead the algorithm to different local solutions rather than a global optimum.

b. Use BFGS to minimize f(x) twice, starting at x(0) = -15 and x(0) = 0. Supply f'(x) to the optimizer. For each run, report the final value of x, the final objective value, |f'(x)|, and whether the optimizer reported convergence.

result_1 <- optim(
  par = -15,
  fn = f,
  gr = f_prime,
  method = "BFGS"
)

result_2 <- optim(
  par = 0,
  fn = f,
  gr = f_prime,
  method = "BFGS"
)

x1 <- result_1$par
f1 <- result_1$value
grad1 <- abs(f_prime(x1))
converged1 <- result_1$convergence == 0

x2 <- result_2$par
f2 <- result_2$value
grad2 <- abs(f_prime(x2))
converged2 <- result_2$convergence == 0

results <- data.frame(
  Starting_Value = c(-15, 0),
  Final_x = c(x1, x2),
  Final_Objective = c(f1, f2),
  Absolute_Gradient = c(grad1, grad2),
  Converged = c(converged1, converged2)
)

results

BFGS can converge to different points because the objective is nonconvex and the algorithm is not guaranteed to find the global optimum from every starting point. The run starting from a more negative value may settle at a lower region of the function. The lower objective value identifies the better local minimum, but additional evidence such as multiple starting values, derivative checks, or a global search would be needed before declaring it the global minimum.

c. Explain why the two runs can converge to different answers. Which run gives the lower objective value? What additional evidence would be needed before claiming that this point is the global minimum?

The two runs can converge to different answers because BFGS is a local optimization method. In a nonconvex function, the path followed by the optimizer depends on the starting point, so it may end in different basins of attraction. The run starting from a more negative value gives the lower objective value. Before claiming it is the global minimum, one would need additional evidence such as a grid search, multiple random starts, or a global optimization method to verify that no lower value exists elsewhere.

The coefficients of x1 and x6 can change substantially even when the fitted values change very little. This happens because in nearly collinear designs, the model can move the coefficient mass between the two highly similar predictors while holding the summed effect nearly constant. The root mean squared difference and maximum absolute difference between the fitted-value vectors remain small, indicating that the combined effect is stable but the decomposition into separate effects is not.

c. Use the identity y* − y = 10(x6 − x1) to explain why the fitted values change very little while the two coefficients change substantially. What does this example suggest about interpreting separate effects for nearly collinear predictors?

Because x6 is nearly identical to x1, the difference x6 − x1 is tiny. Therefore, y* − y = 10(x6 − x1) is a small perturbation, so the fitted values only change a little. However, the coefficient estimates are not separately identifiable in a stable way: the model can allocate the same total effect between x1 and x6 in different ways, causing large coefficient changes even when the fitted values remain almost the same. This example shows that separate effects for nearly collinear predictors are often difficult to interpret reliably.

Question 6 (Can 39 million Fitbit records represent US adults?)

Patten et al. (2026) describe Fitbit data from 59,018 participants in the All of Us Research Program. The dataset spans 14 years and contains more than 39 million daily step records. Participants contributed data through one of two routes:

  • Bring Your Own Device (BYOD): participants shared data from a Fitbit they already owned.
  • Wearables Enhancing All of Us Research (WEAR): invited participants received a Fitbit at no cost.

In the general activity cohort, the BYOD and WEAR groups contained 32,035 and 22,474 participants, respectively. The BYOD value is listed first in each comparison below:

  • 77.3% versus 55.1% reported being White;
  • 6.1% versus 15.2% reported annual household income between $10,000 and $25,000; and
  • median daily steps were 6,867 versus 5,797.

Suppose the target is the mean of the participant-specific average daily step counts among US adults during the study period, with each adult given equal weight. A researcher writes:

“This dataset contains more than 39 million daily Fitbit records. Therefore, the average of all recorded step counts should provide an accurate estimate of mean daily activity among US adults.”

a. Are the 39 million daily records independent observations? What characteristics or behaviors could cause some participants to contribute more recorded days than others? Explain how averaging all recorded days would then weight participants unequally.

No. The 39 million daily records are not independent observations because many records come from the same participants. Some participants may contribute more recorded days than others because they used the device for longer, shared more data, wore it consistently, or had fewer missing recordings. If one participant contributes many more days than another, then averaging all daily records gives that person more weight. This does not match the target, which gives each adult equal weight.

b. BYOD participants reported a median of 6,867 daily steps, compared with 5,797 among WEAR participants. Does this comparison show that already owning a Fitbit causes people to walk more? Explain your answer and give at least one plausible alternative explanation based on how participants entered the two groups.

No. The higher median for BYOD participants does not show that owning a Fitbit causes people to walk more. Participants were not randomly assigned to BYOD or WEAR. BYOD participants already owned a Fitbit and chose to share their data, while WEAR participants were invited to participate and received a Fitbit at no cost. BYOD participants may have already been more interested in fitness or physical activity, so the observed difference could reflect selection rather than causation.

c. A very large dataset can still produce a biased estimate of a population quantity when the people and observations entering the dataset are selected. Using this study, explain how selection of both participants and recorded days could make the observed data differ from the US adult population. Why might the naive average of all recorded daily step counts fail to estimate the stated target? You do not need to determine the direction of the bias.

A large dataset does not eliminate selection bias. The participants who contributed Fitbit data may differ from the overall US adult population, and the number of recorded days also varies across participants. Those who contribute more days are weighted more heavily in a naive average of daily step counts, so the resulting estimate can differ from the target population quantity, which is defined as the mean of participant-specific average daily steps with equal weight per adult. The observed sample may overrepresent certain subgroups or behaviors, and the naive average may therefore fail to estimate the target even though the dataset is large.

Source: Patten, T., Preble, E. A., Master, H., et al. (2026). The All of Us Research Program's wearables dataset. Nature Medicine, 32, 2302–2310.
